Neural networks explained

Perceptrons the atomic unit of neural networks

One artificial neuron can only draw one straight line. Stack enough of them and you get a core part of every large language model.

Four points on a grid. Two are positive, two are negative. No single straight line separates them.

That's XOR. In 1969, Minsky and Papert wrote down the math behind limits like this one, and their book fed into a decline in neural network research.4 Yet a close cousin of that unit sits inside every large language model (LLM) today.

This piece follows that unit from one neuron to a deep network,13 and asks what depth gives you that width doesn't. Depth means more layers. Width means more neurons per layer.

The short answerOne neuron can only draw a straight line. Enough neurons in layers can draw almost any shape, and in theory one middle layer is already enough. What extra layers buy you is efficiency: some shapes need far fewer neurons when the network is deep. Whether training can find those deep solutions is less clear, and you can test it yourself near the end.

Why care? This unit is still the main building block of today's AI. GPT-3 has about 175 billion weights,8 and as a rough rule, writing each chunk of text takes about one multiply-and-add per weight. Knowing what one unit can and can't do explains why networks have layers at all, and why deeper ones won out.

01

What does one perceptron do?

A perceptron makes a yes-or-no call from a few numbers. Say you're deciding whether to take an umbrella, based on two signals: is it cloudy, and does the forecast say rain? Each signal gets a weight, which says how much it counts. The perceptron multiplies each input by its weight, adds them up, then adds one more number, the bias, which sets how easily it says yes. If the total is above zero, it outputs 1 (yes). Otherwise, 0 (no).

Frank Rosenblatt introduced it in 1958.1 A neural network is many of these units wired together in layers, each layer feeding the next.

It helps to picture this as a line. With two inputs, every possible input is a point on a flat grid. The points where w1*x1 + w2*x2 + b is exactly zero form a straight line. On one side, the perceptron says 1. On the other, 0. With more inputs, the line becomes a flat sheet in more dimensions, called a hyperplane. Either way, one perceptron makes one straight cut.

How does it learn?

It learns from labeled examples, shown one at a time:

  • If it gets the example right, change nothing.
  • If it says 0 when the label is 1, add the input to the weights and 1 to the bias.
  • If it says 1 when the label is 0, subtract the input from the weights and 1 from the bias.

Each fix moves the line so that example is more likely to land on the right side. That's the whole method. One pass through all the examples is called an epoch.

In 1962, Novikoff proved the rule always works on one condition: some straight line must split the two groups. If one exists, the rule finds one after a limited number of fixes.2

When no such line exists, the proof promises nothing. You can see both cases below. On OR (1 if either input is 1), the rule settles. On XOR, it never does. The page remembers the weights at every step. If the same weights come back at the same example, the rule is in a loop it will repeat forever, and the page says so.

So one neuron is one straight line. What happens when a straight line isn't enough?

02

Why can't one neuron learn XOR?

XOR means "one or the other, but not both." Think of a hallway light with a switch at each end: flip either switch and the light changes. Its four cases are (0,0), (0,1), (1,0) and (1,1), with answers 0, 1, 1, 0. On a grid, the two 1s sit on one diagonal and the two 0s on the other.

Try to draw one straight line with both 1s on one side and both 0s on the other. You can't. Any line that catches both 1s also catches at least one 0.

So the learning rule never settles on XOR. The rule isn't broken: there's no right answer to find. The data is not linearly separable, meaning no straight line splits it.

Minsky and Papert's 1969 book Perceptrons worked out the math of limits like this one.4 XOR is the tidy example, but the limit is real. Real datasets can fail the straight-line test too, and then a single unit can't sort them perfectly, however long it trains.

Try it below: a line can split AND and OR, but not XOR. The Rings preset is closer to real data, with one group sitting inside the other, like health readings that are fine near normal and worrying once they drift too far in any direction. No line splits that either.

If one line can't do it, what about two?

03

How do three neurons solve it?

The fix was known early: one neuron gives one line, so use several and combine their lines. For XOR, three are enough. Two sit in a hidden layer, between the inputs and the output. All three use the same yes-or-no step rule:

  • Hidden neuron 1 computes OR: step(x1 + x2 - 0.5)
  • Hidden neuron 2 computes NAND: step(-x1 - x2 + 1.5)
  • The output neuron computes AND of the two: step(h1 + h2 - 1.5)

The first hidden neuron fires if at least one input is on. The second fires unless both are on. The output fires only when both hidden neurons fire. That leaves exactly the two cases where one input is on.

On the grid, each hidden neuron draws a line, and the output keeps the strip between them.

The right-hand panel shows a second view. The hidden layer moves each input to a new spot, (h1, h2). There, (0,1) and (1,0) both land on (1,1), and now one straight line does split the groups. The output neuron is an ordinary perceptron drawing that line.

That works when you pick the weights yourself. The hard part was getting a network to find them on its own.

04

Why was a network like this so hard to train?

Picking weights by hand works for XOR. Real problems need the weights learned from data, and the perceptron rule can't do that for hidden neurons. It fixes weights based on the output's mistake. A hidden neuron's effect on the output passes through other neurons, so the rule can't tell how much of the blame is its own.

Widrow and Hoff got part of the way in 1959.3 They scored the error as a number, the squared gap between output and target, and nudged the weights in whichever direction shrinks it fastest. That direction is the gradient. Following it step by step, like walking downhill on the error, is called gradient descent. Bigger mistakes mean bigger steps. Their method, least mean squares (LMS), works for one neuron.

With two layers, it breaks. To get the gradient for a hidden weight, you use the chain rule: multiply the slopes along the path from that weight to the output. A step function is flat everywhere except at its jump, so its slope is zero (and has no value at the jump). One zero in the product makes the whole gradient zero. The error surface is flat ground and sudden cliffs, with no downhill to follow.

The fix was small: swap the step for a smooth S-shaped curve, the sigmoid, which has a slope everywhere. Now the chain rule reaches every layer, and you get backpropagation, which passes blame backward from the output to every weight.

Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams published it in 19865 and called it the generalized delta rule, an extension of Widrow and Hoff's delta rule.6 The idea is older, going back at least to Werbos's 1974 thesis.7

Below, the XOR network trains from random starting weights. Pick step to see the problem: the gradient is worked out exactly, comes out zero, and the weights never move.

That small fix still sits at the heart of today's biggest models.

05

Is this still how modern models work?

Yes, only far bigger. GPT-3 has 96 layers. Each has an attention block, which lets each word draw on the words before it, and an MLP block.8 The MLP block is a two-layer perceptron network with 49,152 hidden units and 12,288 outputs. Our XOR network has three neurons.

Modern models use smoother activations, such as GELU or ReLU, instead of a step. The recipe hasn't changed: weighted sums, a bend, repeat.

So networks grew by stacking layers. But did they have to?

06

Is one hidden layer enough?

If you can stack perceptrons, how many layers do you need?

In 1989, Cybenko proved that one is enough, in principle.9 A network with a single hidden layer of sigmoid units can match any continuous curve (one with no jumps) on a compact domain (think of a closed range like 0 to 1) as closely as you like. In 1991, Hornik showed the same holds for many other activations.10 This is the universal approximation theorem. Taken at its word, it says you never need depth.

What doesn't the theorem tell you?

It says good weights exist. It says nothing about two practical questions:

  1. How many neurons? The hidden layer may need to be huge. For some functions with many inputs, far bigger than anything you could build.
  2. Can you find the weights? Knowing they exist doesn't mean gradient descent will reach them.

The figure pulls those two apart. Build by hand sets up a one-hidden-layer network from n units, with no training. Learn gives the same network random weights and trains it with gradient descent. If Learn does worse with the same number of units, the network could have done it; training didn't find the way.

If one layer is enough in theory, why go deep?

07

So what does depth buy you?

Two papers from 2016 put numbers on it. In plain terms:

  • Eldan and Shamir found a shape, rings spreading out from a center like ripples on a pond, that a small network with three layers can draw. A network with two layers can't get close unless it is enormous, and the size it needs grows exponentially as you add input dimensions.11
  • Telgarsky stacked a simple zigzag piece many times. Each new layer roughly doubles the number of zigzags. A network with far fewer layers can only copy the result if it has a huge number of neurons. Stacking multiplies. Widening only adds.12
The exact statements

Eldan and Shamir (2016): there is a simple, roughly radial function on ℝd that a small three-layer network can express. No two-layer network approximates it beyond a constant accuracy unless its width is exponential in d.

Telgarsky (2016): for every positive integer k, some networks with Θ(k³) layers and Θ(1) nodes per layer can't be approximated by networks with O(k) layers unless those have Ω(2k) nodes. It holds for ReLU and several other unit types.

IntuitionWhy would depth help? The usual answer is reuse. Each layer builds on the patterns the layer before it found, the way words build on letters. A shallow network has to build every pattern straight from the raw input. This is a way to think about the theorems, not something they prove.

Can you see it in a small example?

My constructionThis example is my own, not from those papers. It uses ReLU, a common activation that outputs max(0, z): it passes positive numbers through and turns negative ones into 0. A "tent" made of two ReLU units, tent(x) = 2·relu(x) − 4·relu(x − ½), folds the range 0 to 1 in half like a sheet of paper. Feed the result into a second tent and each half folds again.

Each block doubles the straight pieces, so k blocks use 2k units and make 2k pieces. One hidden layer of n ReLU units can make at most n + 1 pieces on a single input, since each unit adds at most one bend. Slide k to compare.

08

The short version

The universal approximation theorem answers whether a network can draw a shape. Depth is about how cheaply it can, and whether training finds the weights. The first question has a simple answer; the second is where most of the interesting work in deep learning happens.

Open questionStarting from random weights, does a deep network learn to build the zigzag fold by fold, or does training get stuck?

What can't these demos tell you?

Everything here uses tiny networks trained in your browser on one or two inputs. They show how the pieces work, not how they behave at scale. Each experiment uses only a few random starts, so a gap between two settings can be luck. That's why the full experiment shows the lowest and highest result next to the average.

How the numbers are computed

  1. Every number in the figures is computed live in your browser, in double-precision JavaScript. Nothing is precomputed or stored. Constants in the text and diagrams (dates, GPT-3 sizes) come from the sources above.
  2. Randomness comes from a seeded generator (mulberry32), so the same seed and settings give the same result on any machine.   3. Gradients are exact backpropagation. A test suite checks them against finite differences, checks that a step network's gradients are exactly zero, that k stacked tent blocks give 2k pieces, that the construct mode passes through every knot, and that the perceptron converges on OR and cycles on XOR.
